The Limits of Understanding

William James, On a Certain Blindness in Human Beings (1899)

How much of another person's experience can we really understand? James explores our tendency to judge the meaning of other people's lives through the narrow lens of our own perspective.

Why this follows Plato: Escaping one limited perspective does not guarantee that we can see through another person's eyes. Intellectual humility may begin with recognizing what remains beyond our understanding.

Field Journal question: Can we genuinely understand another person's experience, or is learning to respect what we cannot understand more important?

A productive tension: When does respecting another person's perspective become an excuse for avoiding necessary moral judgment?

Shadows on the Wall

Plato, The Republic, Book VII · translated by Benjamin Jowett

And now, I said, let me show in a figure how far our nature is enlightened or unenlightened:—Behold! human beings living in a underground den...

Plato asks us to imagine people who have lived their entire lives in a cave, seeing only shadows cast on a wall. What they see is real as an experience—but is it the whole of reality?

A question to carry with you: What might we mistake for the whole simply because it is all we have ever known?
